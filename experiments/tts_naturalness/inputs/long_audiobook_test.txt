For a long time I used to go to bed early. Sometimes, when I had put out my candle, my eyes would close so quickly that I had not even time to say "I'm going to sleep." And half an hour later, the thought that it was time to go to bed would wake me up; I would try to put down the book I still had in my hands, and blow out the light. I had gone on thinking, while I was asleep, about what I had just been reading, but these thoughts had taken a peculiar turn; it seemed to me that I myself was the thing the book was about: a church, a quartet, the rivalry between Francis I and Charles V. This belief would persist for a few seconds after I woke up; it did not shock my reason, but it lay heavily on my eyes like scales, and prevented them from realising that the lamp was no longer burning. Then it would begin to be unintelligible, as the thoughts of one who has been reincarnated do; the subject of the book would detach itself from me, and I could reattach myself to it or not, as I chose; and at once I recovered my sight, and I would be astonished to find around me a darkness which was very gentle, very restful to the eyes, and even more, perhaps, to the mind.

I would ask myself what hour it could be; I heard the whistle of the trains, which, now far off, now near, like the song of a bird in a wood, showed me the extent of the silent countryside. The traveller who is on his way to the country through which it runs thinks of the pleasure he will have when he appears before his host, of the joy of being welcomed; and, if it is already late, of the regret of those who are waiting for him. On the left hand, I could see the lines of the windowpanes, whose shape the darkness had only rendered more indistinct.

Then I would turn over, and my gaze would fall on the little night-light which, placed near the head of my bed, sent out only just enough light to prevent me from being able to distinguish clearly the objects in the room. And I would try to go to sleep again, and soon I would be dreaming that I was walking about in the garden of the old house at Combray, or that I was playing at being a soldier, or that I was having dinner with the Swanns, or that I was walking along the road to Méséglise, and then suddenly the memory would come back to me that I was in my bed in Paris, and I would wake up with a start, and would lie there for a little while, smiling at the thought of Combray, and would fall asleep again.

There were nights when, as soon as I had gone to sleep, the dear form of my mother would appear to me, and I would stretch out my arms to embrace her; but then the recollection would come to me that she was dead, and I would wake up, and would lie there, for a long time, motionless, my eyes wide open in the darkness, and would try to go to sleep again, and would think of my mother, and would say to myself that she was looking after me, that she loved me, that she would never leave me, that she was always with me. And then I would go to sleep again, and would dream that I was talking to her, and that she was answering me, and that I was telling her that I loved her, and that I would never leave her again.

When I woke up in the morning, I would hear the sound of the gardener's wheelbarrow going past under my window, and the clink of the spade against the stones, and the voice of the gardener saying good morning to the cook, and then the clatter of the plates and dishes in the kitchen, and I would know that the day had begun. I would get up, and I would go downstairs, and I would find my mother sitting by the fire, with her sewing in her hands, and she would look up at me and smile, and say good morning, and ask me if I had slept well, and then she would give me a cup of hot chocolate, and I would sit down beside her, and we would talk, and I would tell her about my dreams, and she would listen to me, and would smile, and would say that I was a good boy.

And that was the way things were, for a long time. And then one day, something happened that changed everything. It was a Tuesday, I think, in the month of October. I had been playing in the garden, and I had come inside, and I was sitting on the stairs, reading a book, when I heard the bell ring. The cook went to the door, and I heard her speak to someone, and then she came back into the kitchen, and said that there was a gentleman to see my father. And then I heard my father go to the door, and I heard him speak to the gentleman, and then I heard him come back into the house, and he went into his study, and closed the door. And I did not know it then, but that was the day that everything changed. That was the day that the war began.
